\section{State of the Art}

The increasing use of Deep Neural Networks in applications such as
computer vision, speech recognition, and natural language processing
has increased the demand for hardware capable of executing these
models efficiently. Although CPUs and GPUs can execute a wide range of
workloads, their general-purpose nature may not provide the required
energy efficiency for every application. This is especially relevant
in edge and embedded systems, where power consumption, area, and
memory are limited and often tailored to application specific domains and applications (automotive, medical, industrials, etc.).

As a result, many domain-specific DNN accelerators have been proposed.
These accelerators target different environments, from large data
centers to small always-on sensing devices, but they usually exploit a
similar set of ideas. These include spatial and/or temporal parallelism, data management and reuse,
sparsity, and reduced numerical representation/precision. The way in which these
techniques are combined has a direct effect on performance, power,
energy, and area \cite{sze2017efficient}.

\subsection{DNN Hardware Accelerators}

DNN accelerators can be classified according to their compute
organization, dataflow, flexibility, or implementation technology.
These categories are not exclusive, since a single accelerator may
combine ideas from several of them.

\subsubsection{Spatial and Systolic Arrays}

Many accelerators distribute multiply-accumulate operations over an
array of processing elements. Spatial architectures map different
operations and data movements directly onto this array, allowing data
to be reused close to the compute units.

Eyeriss is an early example of a spatial accelerator for convolutional
neural networks \cite{eyeriss}. It uses an array of processing elements
and a row-stationary dataflow designed to reuse weights, activations,
and partial sums. Its architecture shows the importance of the memory
hierarchy and data movement, since the cost of moving data can be as
relevant as the cost of the arithmetic operations themselves.

Systolic arrays are a more regular form of computing array. Data moves
between neighboring processing elements in a pipelined manner, while
each element performs part of a matrix multiplication. Google's first
Tensor Processing Unit is a well-known example of this approach
\cite{tpuv1}. It was designed for neural network inference in data
centers and uses a large matrix multiplication unit supported by
software-managed on-chip memory. The TPU shows how a specialized
architecture can provide high throughput when it is designed around
the dominant operations of a workload.

\subsubsection{Precision-Scalable and Reconfigurable Accelerators}

Different accelerators use diverse and even mixed data representation (integer, fixed, floating point) and support several 
types of workloads, according to the DNN solutions that can be mapped on them. Some architectures introduce reconfigurability to
adapt their precision, dataflow, topology, parallelism level, or power consumption.

Envision explores another part of the accelerator design space
\cite{envision}. It supports different numerical precisions and adapts
its voltage and operating frequency to trade accuracy and performance
for energy efficiency. It also exploits sparsity to avoid unnecessary
operations. This illustrates that the optimal accelerator
configuration can depend not only on the DNN model, but also on the
precision and operating conditions selected by both the AI solutions architects at the Edge AI level where they apply quantization techniques and the chip designers.

TinyVers targets the opposite end of the power and performance range
\cite{tinyvers}. It is an ultra-low-power system-on-chip intended for
machine learning inference at the extreme edge. It combines a RISC-V
processor, a reconfigurable machine learning accelerator, on-chip
memory, and state-retentive eMRAM. Its design shows that flexibility,
power management, and memory organization are especially important in
small always-on devices.

\subsubsection{Sparsity-Aware Accelerators}

Sparsity provides another opportunity to reduce both computation and
data movement. Neural networks can contain zero-valued weights and
activations either naturally or as a result of techniques such as
pruning (and quantization). Hardware can exploit this property by
avoiding operations and memory transfers associated with zero values.

SCNN is an example of an accelerator designed specifically for sparse
convolutional neural networks \cite{scnn}. It stores both weights and
activations in compressed form and organizes computation around their
non-zero values, reducing unnecessary multiply-accumulate operations
and data movement. However, sparse execution is less regular than
dense execution and introduces additional control and data-management
requirements. The resulting benefit therefore depends on both the
amount of sparsity present in the workload and the hardware support
used to exploit it.

\subsubsection{Depth-First and Heterogeneous Accelerators}

Accelerators can also differ in the order in which layers are
executed. DepFiN uses depth-first execution and layer fusion to reduce
the external memory traffic produced by intermediate feature maps in
high-resolution convolutional networks \cite{depfin}. Instead of
completing one full layer before starting the following one, the
accelerator processes parts of several layers in sequence, allowing
intermediate data to remain closer to the compute units.

Another distinction can be made between digital accelerators and
designs based on analog or in-memory computing. Digital designs are
usually easier to control and provide predictable numerical results,
while analog approaches can offer greater compute density at the cost
of other accuracy and implementation constraints.

DIANA presents a heterogeneous approach for edge inference
\cite{diana}. It combines a conventional RISC-V host with both a
digital DNN accelerator and an analog in-memory computing core. The
digital core provides flexibility and deterministic computation,
while the analog core targets high computational density and energy
efficiency. This is an example of how different hardware
implementations can be combined to cover workloads with different
requirements.

\subsubsection{The complex problem}

The accelerators shown illustrate that there is no single architecture 
optimal for every DNN and every target/application platform for its chip implementation even once a given DNN solution has been fixed by Edge AI deployment steps that provided the required AI accuracy performance. 
Decisions such as the
number and organization of the processing elements, the memory sizes
and their bandwidths, the global and local dataflows, the numerical representation, and the use of
sparsity create a large design space. Exploring this space manually is
difficult, which has motivated the development of automated design
space exploration tools.

\subsection{Architecture and Mapping Exploration}

Design Space Exploration tools evaluate different accelerator
configurations without requiring a complete hardware implementation
for each candidate. These tools use analytical or simulation-based
models to estimate metrics such as latency, energy, and area. This
makes it possible to discard unsuitable alternatives early in the
design process.

The cost model used by a DSE framework determines a trade-off between
evaluation speed and implementation detail. Analytical models can
evaluate large numbers of candidates quickly, while more detailed
simulation or implementation-based approaches can capture additional
hardware effects at a greater computational cost. For example,
cycle-accurate simulation can model the execution of hardware
components in more detail, while approaches based on High-Level
Synthesis can use an implementation of the candidate to obtain more
technology-dependent estimates. The appropriate level of abstraction
therefore depends on the size of the design space and the required
accuracy of the exploration.

ZigZag is a framework for the joint exploration of DNN accelerator
architectures and mappings \cite{zigzag}. It represents the processing
array and memory hierarchy and evaluates how the operations and data
of a workload are mapped onto them. ZigZag is especially useful for
studying data reuse and the cost of data movement across the different
levels of memory.

MAESTRO provides a data-centric analytical model for evaluating DNN
accelerator dataflows \cite{maestro}. It analyzes how the selected
dataflow and hardware configuration affect data reuse, communication,
performance, and hardware cost. This allows different mapping
strategies to be compared without requiring a complete implementation
of each accelerator.

Timeloop provides another framework for systematically evaluating DNN
accelerator mappings \cite{timeloop}. It uses a loop-based description
of the workload, architecture, and mapping to explore how computations
and data movement are distributed across the accelerator memory
hierarchy. It is commonly combined with Accelergy
\cite{accelergy}, which provides analytical energy estimates for the
different hardware components and their accesses. Together,
Timeloop and Accelergy allow mappings to be evaluated in terms of
performance and energy over flexible accelerator descriptions.

ConfuciuX addresses accelerator exploration from a different
perspective \cite{confuciux}. Given a DNN model and a dataflow style,
it searches for suitable assignments of compute and memory resources
while considering constraints such as area or power. The exploration
uses reinforcement learning to guide the search over the large
hardware resource space and can apply a genetic algorithm to further
refine the resulting configurations. In this way, ConfuciuX
demonstrates how learning-based search techniques can be used to
explore hardware configurations without exhaustively evaluating the
complete design space.

These frameworks differ both in the hardware configurations they can
represent and in the mapping spaces they explore. In particular,
ZigZag extends the mapping space by supporting uneven mappings, where
different operands can use different loop blocking across shared
memory levels. This increases the number of possible mappings but can
also expose solutions that are not represented by more restrictive
mapping models.

However, this type of exploration works mainly with an abstract
description of the architecture. Parameters such as memory capacity,
bandwidth, access energy, and area describe the expected hardware, but
they do not necessarily identify the concrete hardware IP blocks that
will implement it. Therefore, an architecture that appears promising
at this level may be more difficult to implement with the components
available for a given technology.

\subsection{Automatic Generation of DNN Accelerator IPs}

Other approaches go further and attempt to automate part of the
hardware generation process.

MAGNet is a modular accelerator generator that combines accelerator
exploration with a hardware generation flow \cite{magnet}. Instead of
exploring an unrestricted hardware architecture, it uses a
parameterizable accelerator template and modifies design parameters
such as the SIMD organization and memory sizes. Candidate designs can
then be evaluated through an HLS-based implementation flow. This
provides a closer connection between architectural decisions and
implementable hardware, although the exploration remains constrained
by the underlying accelerator template.

AutoDNNChip combines a Chip Predictor with a Chip Builder to explore
accelerator configurations and produce hardware implementations for
FPGA and ASIC targets \cite{autodnnchip}. Its design representation
includes architecture, mapping, and hardware IP information in a
common graph. The Chip Predictor supports both a coarse-grained
analytical mode for fast exploration and a fine-grained run-time
simulation mode for more detailed evaluation. The Chip Builder uses
these models to explore aspects such as IP selection, block
configuration, resource allocation, and inter-IP pipelining before
generating synthesizable hardware.

AutoAI2C follows a similar generator-oriented direction and focuses on
automatically producing DNN accelerators for both FPGA and ASIC
platforms \cite{autoai2c}.

These approaches reduce the manual effort required to move from a DNN
model to a hardware design. However, automatic generation also
requires stronger assumptions about the supported architecture,
target platform, component library, and generation flow. This can make
the relationship between the exploration model and the final hardware
implementation more tightly coupled.

A different line of research uses Large Language Models to generate
or assist in the generation of HDL code. DeepRTL proposes a unified
representation for Verilog understanding and generation
\cite{deeprtl}, while RTLCoder uses an open-source dataset and a
specialized model for RTL generation \cite{liu2025rtlcoder}. These
works are relevant to hardware automation, but they address a
different problem from architectural DSE. Their main objective is the
generation of HDL descriptions rather than the systematic exploration
of accelerator architectures and hardware IP combinations.

\subsection{Summary of the chapter}

The reviewed works cover several parts of the accelerator design
process. Accelerators such as Eyeriss, SAURIA, TinyVers, and DIANA
demonstrate the diversity of architectural and implementation
decisions found in DNN accelerator design. A comparison of their
technology, numerical precision, compute organization, and main
architectural features is provided in
Table~\ref{tab:dnn_accelerator_comparison}. Exploration and
generation frameworks are compared separately in
Table~\ref{tab:dnn_tool_comparison}.
Exploration frameworks such as MAESTRO, Timeloop with Accelergy,
ConfuciuX, and ZigZag allow mappings, accelerator architectures, or
hardware resource allocations to be evaluated before implementation.
Generator-oriented approaches such as MAGNet, AutoDNNChip, and
AutoAI2C attempt to continue the process towards a concrete hardware
design.

Some of these approaches already incorporate implementation
information during exploration. In particular, AutoDNNChip includes
technology-dependent hardware IP information and performs IP
selection as part of its accelerator generation flow. However,
architecture, mapping, and IP decisions are represented and optimized
within the same integrated generation framework.

Talos follows a different organization by explicitly separating these
decisions into two exploration levels. Its first level explores
abstract DNN accelerator architectures using ZigZag, without binding
them to a particular implementation of their hardware components.
Its second level then maps the selected architectures to compatible,
characterized hardware IP blocks and evaluates their PPA
characteristics. This separation is intended to allow the
architectural exploration and the hardware IP library to evolve
independently, while still considering implementation information
before a final candidate is selected. Talos also returns the mapping
generated by ZigZag for each selected architecture.
