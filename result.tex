\section{Results}

This section evaluates the implemented Talos flow using the final
exploration results. The objective of these experiments is not only to
find the minimum value of a single metric, but also to show the type of
accelerator configurations that are obtained when energy, area and
performance are considered together.

The section is organized in three parts. First, a complete INT16
AlexNet exploration is used as an end-to-end proof of concept of the
framework. Then, the Talos configurations are compared against three
published accelerators, Eyeriss v1, Eyeriss v2 and SAURIA. Each
comparison uses the same workload, or the closest one available, as
the one used to evaluate the corresponding accelerator, so that the
differences come from the design and not from the network. Finally,
the architectural estimates produced by Talos are compared against
synthesized implementations of the selected designs.

\subsection{Experimental Setup}

All final experiments use the hierarchical exploration flow described
in the previous chapter. Level~1 explores the abstract architecture
using NSGA-II and ZigZag, while Level~2 selects characterized hardware
IPs and evaluates the resulting physical implementation.

The final comparisons use four workload configurations:

\begin{itemize}
    \item a complete AlexNet network using INT16 operands, used as the
    main proof-of-concept exploration;
    \item the five convolutional layers of AlexNet using INT16
    operands and a batch size of four, reproducing the setup reported
    for Eyeriss v1, used for comparison with that accelerator;
    \item MobileNet V1 0.5/128 using INT8 operands, used for comparison
    with the dense Eyeriss v2 result;
    \item VGG-16 using FP16 operands, used for comparison with SAURIA.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Characterized Hardware IP Pools}

The Level~2 exploration does not use generic analytical costs for the
on-chip components. Instead, it selects implementations from
characterized hardware IP pools. Three different compute pools are
used in the experiments, matching the numerical representation of
each workload: INT8, INT16, and FP16.

All IPs were characterized using Cadence Genus after mapping the RTL
to a TSMC 65~nm standard-cell library. The common characterization
conditions were a reference frequency of 200~MHz, a supply voltage of
1.2~V, a temperature of 25~$^\circ$C, and the NCCOM process corner.
Power was obtained using the vectorless characterization flow
described in the previous chapter. For every component, Talos stores
its area, timing information, idle and active power, and, when
applicable, the corresponding operation or memory-access energy.

The register-file pool ranges from 64 to 2048 bits and provides
bandwidths between 8 and 256 bits per cycle. The three global-buffer
alternatives correspond to 8192, 32768, and 131072 bits, with
bandwidths of 64, 256, and 1024 bits per cycle, respectively.
Level~2 does not necessarily consider all of these implementations
for every architecture. Capacity and bandwidth requirements inherited
from Level~1 are used to discard incompatible memories before the
physical objective is evaluated.

External DRAM is modeled separately from the on-chip accelerator. For
this reason, the tables report both on-chip energy and power and, when
available, the corresponding values including the modeled DRAM
contribution. This distinction is particularly important when comparing
Talos against published chips, since most accelerator papers report
chip power but do not provide the energy of the complete external
memory system.

The Level~2 objectives are denoted as E, A, and P. E minimizes energy
per inference, A minimizes accelerator area, and P favors workload
performance. Their combinations correspond to multi-objective selection
using the scoring method described in the previous chapter.

The results show the expected conflict between the three objectives,
so the single-objective points are compared here on all the metrics
of Table~\ref{tab:alexnet_int16_talos}.
Figure~\ref{fig:pareto_alexnet_int16} plots the same seven
configurations in the latency--area--energy space: the single-objective
points sit at the extremes of the curve, while the combined objectives
fall in between. A+P and E+A+P almost coincide, since they share the
same area and latency, 1.701~mm$^2$ and 22272482 cycles, and only
differ in on-chip energy, 51.819 against 51.142~mJ, a 1.3\% difference
that is not visible on a logarithmic axis. The energy-only
configuration obtains the lowest on-chip and total energy, 39.757 and
112.869~mJ per inference, and a latency of 6332862 cycles, only
improved by the performance-oriented points (3315424 cycles for the
performance-only configuration and 3942051 cycles for the
energy-performance one), but it requires 5.111~mm$^2$ and 1.256~W of
on-chip power. The area-only
configuration is the opposite case: it reduces the accelerator to
0.202~mm$^2$ and 0.045~W, but its latency grows to 248021034 cycles,
39 times the energy-only value, its on-chip energy rises to 55.723~mJ
and its total energy to 450.138~mJ, four times more, because the idle
energy on chip and, above all, the DRAM energy accumulate during the
whole execution. The performance-only configuration reaches the lowest
latency, 3315424 cycles, but at the cost of 1493.550~mm$^2$, 280.541~W
and 4650.562~mJ of on-chip energy per inference.

The performance-only result shows why a performance objective should
not normally be used without an area or energy constraint. Talos keeps
increasing the available parallel hardware until it obtains the lowest
latency in the explored set. This point is not a realistic accelerator
target, but it is useful to verify that the exploration is correctly
exposing the trade-off instead of hiding it behind a fixed
architecture template.

The combined objectives produce more useful designs. In particular,
the E+A+P configuration uses 1.701~mm$^2$, consumes 51.142~mJ of
on-chip energy per inference, and completes the workload in 22272482
cycles. It is not the best point in any single metric, but it avoids the
very large area of the performance-oriented configurations and the very
large latency of the area-oriented configurations. The A+P and E+A+P
solutions also converge to the same area and latency, while including
energy in the objective slightly improves both the on-chip and total
energy. This configuration is therefore used as the representative
balanced design in the later synthesis validation.

Another result visible in Table~\ref{tab:alexnet_int16_talos} is the
importance of the external memory contribution. For the E+A+P design,
the on-chip energy is 51.142~mJ per inference, while the complete model
including DRAM reaches 198.234~mJ. The difference is even larger for
some of the area-oriented points. This is why the following comparisons
keep on-chip and total figures separated whenever the published
accelerator does not report DRAM energy.

\subsection{Comparison with Published Accelerators}

The next experiments compare Talos against three published DNN
accelerators. A different workload is used for each comparison in order
to reproduce the network, precision, and operating conditions reported
by the corresponding paper as closely as possible.

These comparisons should still be interpreted as reference points
rather than exact chip-to-chip benchmarks. Talos uses characterized RTL
blocks and analytical workload activity, while the published results
come from fabricated accelerators or post-layout implementations.
Voltage, memory system assumptions, physical design effort, and the
exact definition of reported power can also differ between works.

\subsubsection{Eyeriss v1: AlexNet INT16}
Eyeriss v1 provides the closest comparison for the INT16 flow because
both designs use a 65~nm technology, 16-bit integer arithmetic and a
200~MHz core frequency. For this experiment, the Talos workload is not
the AlexNet model from the previous subsection. Instead, it
reproduces the five AlexNet convolutional layers and the batch-size-four
setup used by Eyeriss. Talos energy and latency are normalized per
image. However, the power and energy columns must be read with one
caveat: the Talos IPs were characterized at the nominal 1.2~V supply
of the TSMC 65~nm LP library, whereas the Eyeriss v1 figures were
measured with the chip running at 1.0~V \cite{chen2017eyeriss}, so
part of the gap between both designs comes from the operating point
and not from the architecture.\footnote{Since dynamic power scales
with the square of the supply voltage, the voltage difference alone
accounts for a factor of up to $(1.2/1.0)^2\simeq1.44$ in favour of
Eyeriss. Applied to the energy-oriented Talos point, the on-chip
energy ratio would drop from 3.8 to roughly 2.6 and the power ratio
from 4.4 to roughly 3.0. This is a first-order correction: leakage
also changes with the voltage, and the characterized blocks were not
verified to meet 200~MHz at 1.0~V, so the Talos values are reported as
characterized.}

The balanced E+A+P design occupies 3.217~mm$^2$, 73.7\% less than the

This area reduction comes at the cost of energy and performance: Talos
requires 32.521~mJ of on-chip energy per image and 8.876 million cycles,
approximately 4.1 and 1.5 times the respective Eyeriss values.

The energy-oriented point exposes a different trade-off. It occupies
5.111~mm$^2$ and completes an image in 5.000 million cycles, reaching
40.00 inferences/s compared with 34.7 for Eyeriss. It therefore provides
higher performance with less than half the area, but its 30.364~mJ of
on-chip energy is still approximately 3.8 times the Eyeriss value, and
its 1.215~W of on-chip power is approximately 4.4 times the measured
Eyeriss chip power. The custom Eyeriss implementation remains clearly
more energy-efficient for the specific row-stationary AlexNet workload
for which it was designed, but the Talos exploration can identify a
smaller implementation with competitive latency.

produces an extreme design. The P configuration reduces latency to
1.493 million cycles, but does so with an area of 14145.161~mm$^2$ and
268.136~W of on-chip power. The E+P result reaches 1.845 million cycles
with a much smaller, although still large, 30.510~mm$^2$
implementation. This again shows the need to consider area together
with performance during the physical selection stage.

110.515~mm$^2$. In contrast, the balanced E+A+P point reduces the area

inference in 0.721 million cycles. This design is significantly slower
than Eyeriss v2, but represents a much more conservative physical point
in the explored Talos space.

Eyeriss v2 does not provide a complete post-layout area in
mm$^2$ for this implementation. Instead, the paper reports 2695k
NAND-2-equivalent logic gates, excluding SRAM, together with 246\,kB of
on-chip SRAM. Consequently, the physical cost is retained in the
published gate-and-memory form rather than converted to a potentially
misleading silicon-area estimate; note~$^{e}$ of
Table~\ref{tab:eyerissv2_mobilenet_comparison} gives an approximate
conversion with the NAND-2 area of the Talos library.

The MobileNet result also shows that the configuration preferred by a
balanced objective does not need to be the configuration that most
closely reproduces the published accelerator. Talos can instead expose
both alternatives: a point with similar latency to Eyeriss v2 and a
smaller balanced point when area is also considered.

\subsubsection{SAURIA: VGG-16 FP16}
SAURIA is used as the floating-point comparison. Both experiments use
VGG-16 with FP16 arithmetic, but the operating conditions are further
apart than in the two Eyeriss comparisons. Talos uses the characterized
65~nm pool at 1.2~V and 200~MHz, while SAURIA is a post-layout GF22FDX
22~nm implementation with a 0.8~V supply and a maximum frequency of
555~MHz \cite{sauria2023}. As in the Eyeriss v1 comparison, part of the
gap therefore comes from the operating point and not from the
architecture: the supply voltage difference alone accounts for a
factor of up to $(1.2/0.8)^2\simeq2.25$ in dynamic power in favour of
SAURIA, on top of the 65~nm to 22~nm technology gap; both effects are
quantified at the end of this subsection. The SAURIA reference point
is its reported VGG-16 execution time of approximately 164~ms,
obtained with the on-the-fly data feeder and assuming 6.4~GB/s of
external DRAM bandwidth; at 555~MHz this corresponds to approximately
91.02 million cycles and 6.10 inferences/s. The SAURIA benchmark covers
the convolutional and fully-connected layers and excludes pooling and
activation layers, and its energy per inference is not measured
directly but derived from the reported 258~mW and the execution time
(note~$^{c}$ of Table~\ref{tab:vgg16_fp16_sauria_comparison}). For
the VGG-16 exploration, the final E+A+P solution was selected from the
larger exploration pool, which produced the best balanced point over
the complete set of runs.

The VGG-16 exploration presents the same general trade-off observed in
the integer workloads. Figure~\ref{fig:pareto_sauria} shows the seven
selected configurations in the latency--area--energy space. Here the
overlapping pair is E and E+P, and in this case the two points are
different Level~1 architectures that collapse into the same physical
implementation. Both use a 32$\times$32 array, but E has a 128~kbit
global buffer with 512~bit/cycle and 256~bit register files, whereas
E+P has a 64~kbit global buffer with 1024~bit/cycle and 128~bit
register files. Both select the \texttt{pe\_fpnew\_fp16\_fma} datapath
and, for both sets of requirements, the smallest compatible memories
in the FP16 pool are the same 128$\times$1024 global buffer and
8$\times$32 register files, so Level~2 instantiates exactly the same
IPs and the area is identical, 14.450~mm$^2$. The remaining
differences come from the Level~1 mapping: 39.92 against 40.46 million
cycles, and a higher DRAM energy for the smaller global buffer of E+P,
530 against 438~mJ, which is why its total energy is higher despite
its lower latency. The area-only point is very small, at
0.226~mm$^2$, but only processes 0.038 inferences/s. The
performance-only point reaches 6.18 inferences/s, which is close to the
VGG-16 rate derived from the SAURIA execution time, but requires
114.820~mm$^2$ and 26.203~W of on-chip power. It is therefore not a
competitive physical implementation even though it reaches a similar
workload-level throughput.

The balanced E+A+P point is more representative of what Talos is
intended to generate. It occupies 3.641~mm$^2$, consumes 0.947~W of
on-chip power and reaches 1.24 inferences/s. SAURIA is still clearly
better in area, power and performance in absolute terms, with
0.897~mm$^2$, 0.258~W and approximately 6.10 inferences/s. However,
this is also the least direct of the three published-accelerator
comparisons because SAURIA uses a much newer 22~nm technology and a
heavily optimized custom FP16 implementation.

When reading these numbers, the technology gap must be taken into
account. The very small area of the Talos points is obtained in a
65~nm process, whereas SAURIA is implemented in 22~nm. Under ideal
scaling, area grows with the square of the feature size, so the
0.897~mm$^2$ of SAURIA would correspond to approximately
$0.897\times(65/22)^2\simeq7.8$~mm$^2$ in the Talos technology, about
twice the area of the balanced E+A+P point and half the area of the
energy-oriented point. In the same way, the dynamic energy of SAURIA
at 0.8~V and 22~nm would grow by a factor of roughly
$(65/22)\times(1.2/0.8)^2\simeq6.7$ when moved to 65~nm and 1.2~V,
which would place its VGG-16 energy near 280~mJ per inference, still
2.7 times lower than the balanced Talos point. These first-order
estimates do not replace a real port of the design, and the SAURIA
authors themselves resort to scaling equations to compare
accelerators across nodes \cite{sauria2023}. They are given only to
show that the area advantage of SAURIA disappears once the node is
accounted for, while its energy and throughput advantage remains,
mainly because of its 555~MHz operating frequency and its custom FP16
datapath.

The energy-only Talos point reaches 4.94 inferences/s and the E+P point
reaches 5.01 inferences/s, but both require 14.450~mm$^2$ and several
watts of on-chip power. This shows that, for the current characterized
FP16 pool, obtaining a SAURIA-like throughput is possible only by
accepting a much larger physical cost. The result therefore identifies
the FP16 compute implementation as an important target for future
improvement of the available IP pool.

still leaving enough error that the final selected accelerator should be
validated by a conventional implementation flow.

\subsection{Results Summary}
The final experiments show several consistent behaviors across the
three workloads.

First, the Level~2 objective has a strong effect on the resulting
hardware. Energy-only configurations tend to use more hardware to
reduce memory traffic and execution time, while area-only
configurations accept very long execution times. Performance-only
optimization repeatedly produces very large accelerators with high
power consumption. The multi-objective configurations avoid these
extremes and produce more realistic accelerator candidates.

Second, DRAM cannot be ignored when evaluating the generated designs.
For several Talos points, the total energy including the modeled DRAM is
much larger than the energy of the on-chip accelerator. Published
accelerators often do not report this contribution, so separating both
values is necessary to avoid presenting an unfair comparison.

Third, the comparisons with Eyeriss v1, Eyeriss v2 and SAURIA show
that Talos can find competitive performance points, although it does
not match the energy efficiency of accelerators optimized for a
specific workload. For Eyeriss v1, the energy-oriented Talos point
uses less than half the area and reaches 40.00 inferences/s instead of
34.7, but consumes approximately 3.8 times the on-chip energy. For the
dense MobileNet/Eyeriss v2 case, the energy-oriented configuration
reaches a very similar latency, although with higher on-chip energy and
power. The SAURIA comparison shows that the current FP16 pool is still
far from the physical efficiency of a custom 22~nm implementation
operating at 0.8~V and 555~MHz, although part of that gap comes from
the operating point rather than from the architecture.

Finally, the synthesis validation supports the use of characterized IPs
as a second exploration level. Across the four synthesized
configurations, Talos underestimates area by 1.18--3.63\% and
overestimates active power by 10.73--23.12\%. Therefore, the current
framework is best understood as a design-space reduction and selection
tool: it can identify meaningful accelerator candidates using physical
IP information, after which the final few designs can be passed to
synthesis for accurate implementation results.

These experiments complete Milestone~\ref{milestone:m5}. The
methodology and its implementation were validated with four workload
configurations and three characterized hardware pools, the selected
designs were compared with published accelerators and, above all, the
Level~2 estimates were checked against synthesized implementations,
which is the evidence that closes this milestone.
